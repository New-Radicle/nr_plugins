# New Radicle Writing Guide

*Voice, structure, and content standards for long-form pieces. Derived from close reading of three reference texts and refined through drafting "Field Notes on Designing with Emotion and Relationship."*

---

## 1. The reference canon

Three texts define the space we write in. Each models a different temperature; all share the same skeleton.

| Reference | What it models | Temperature |
|---|---|---|
| Agüera y Arcas, *What is Intelligence?* (Antikythera) | Book-scale ambition, scholarly receipts, escalation from mundane detail to metaphysical stakes, wit through captions and understatement | **Cool** — institutional, scholarly |
| Matuschak, *How Might We Learn?* | A layer beneath implementation; design fiction (the "Sam" persona); receipts from shipped work; self-critique before others can; generous steelmanning | **Warm** — craft-researcher, values-explicit |
| Awomosu, *Don't Start With Silicon. Start With Soul.* | Renaming as argument; personal rupture as spine; "receipts" (etymology, the industry's own tools turned against it); griot cadence; aphorisms built to travel | **Hot** — prophetic, polemical, personal |

**Default temperature for New Radicle flagship writing: warm.** Cool for research and reference material; hot reserved for opinion pieces, and only when the receipts can carry the voltage. Whatever the temperature, the nine principles below are constant.

---

## 2. The nine principles

**1. Practitioner authority, first-person entry.** Open inside lived work — a build, a client engagement, a rupture. Never argue from credentials or "studies show" alone. *Rule: only write about what you've touched.*

**2. A stated, contestable thesis.** Not "thoughts on X." One claim a smart reader could disagree with, stated plainly within the first few paragraphs. If you can't write the sentence someone would argue with, the piece isn't ready.

**3. Reframe, don't react.** No news-cycle content. Take a word or frame everyone uses lazily and rebuild it from history, etymology, or mechanism. *Topic-selection test: write only when you can replace the frame, not just add a take to it.*

**4. Concrete before abstract, always.** Big ideas enter through a specific, sensory, checkable instance — a transcript excerpt, a scene, a worked example the reader can verify themselves — then escalate. If the piece asserts that something "felt different" or "was profound," show the moment instead. The reader should be able to reach the conclusion before you state it.

**5. Coin portable vocabulary.** Name your stages, patterns, and distinctions (Infrastructure → Persona → Worldview; "emotional unit tests"). Named concepts are the artifact readers carry away and the way the piece propagates without you. Prefer names that evolve existing tech vocabulary over names that import baggage (we chose "worldview" over "philosophy" deliberately).

**6. Receipts matched to register.** Every bold claim carries shown work: named studies with sample sizes, primary sources, links, your own build or data. **Never keep a citation you cannot verify** — an unverifiable attribution converts to a first-person observation or gets cut. Maintain a "sources to link" list at the bottom of every draft until publication.

**7. Visible, generous structure.** Titled sections that could each stand as a claim. Aphorisms get their own line or short paragraph — never buried mid-wall-of-text. One idea per paragraph; if a paragraph is doing five jobs, it's five paragraphs. Length is earned by density; structure is the courtesy that makes length tolerable.

**8. Charitable opposition.** Name the strongest version of the objection, concede what's true in it, then reframe. Never a takedown; always "yes, and here's the bigger frame." Criticize your own work before anyone else can.

**9. Endings that open.** Every piece connects to a continuing practice — a pilot, an experiment, an invitation, a "we'll report back." Content is a node in a body of work, not a standalone artifact. Where possible, close the ring: let the ending pay off an image or word planted in the opening.

## 3. Historical and cultural literacy

The single most distinctive move available to us — modeled most fully by Awomosu — is **genealogy as argument**: showing that the thing tech treats as unprecedented has been run before. This works in two directions at once:

- **It legitimizes.** "Isn't designing with emotion manipulative?" → society already licenses thousands of professionals to do deliberate emotional work (therapy); the question isn't whether, but under what conditions.
- **It disciplines.** The older tradition learned the guardrails the hard way — supervision, licensure, duty of care. Naming the precedent imports its accountability standards, not just its permission.

### The five moves

1. **Etymology.** Trace a word from its root to its narrowed present (*philanthrōpia* → the DAF apparatus; *intelligence* → surveillance). The drift itself is evidence of what power did to the concept. Works especially well as an opening or an ending.
2. **Precedent traditions.** Find the 500-year-old version of the "new" product category (Ignatian discernment and Maimonides' ladder as values-discovery protocols). Reframes the work from novelty to lineage — and imports the tradition's discipline.
3. **Two lineages.** When a capability has both an extractive and a caring ancestor (Bernays vs. Rogers), name both honestly. This inoculates the piece against its own obvious critique and turns "is this good or bad?" into "which lineage will we inherit?" — a design decision.
4. **Cross-cultural receipt.** One concrete example that the reader can feel (Ifaluk *fago*, Japanese *amae*) beats a paragraph of "cultural variation is large." Use it to make abstract claims about universality falsifiable.
5. **Periodization analogy.** Locate the present moment on a known historical curve ("UX design was the same size in 1995"; "where security auditing was before SOC 2"). Tells the reader where we are and what happens next.

### Draw from non-majority power traditions — a standing commitment

When reaching for historical and cultural anchors, **actively prioritize traditions outside the Western/Northern canon**: indigenous knowledge systems, Global South scholarship and practice, Eastern philosophical and contemplative traditions, African and diasporic thought, feminist and oral traditions. Reasons this is a content strategy and not just a value:

- The Western canon is the default everyone else cites; drawing from elsewhere is where the *unclaimed* insight lives. (Awomosu's Ọ̀rúnmìlà–Socrates comparison, via Sophie Oluwole, does argumentative work no additional Greek citation could.)
- Much of what agents now need — relational intelligence, oral transmission, embodied knowledge, collective decision-making, reading emotion in context — was developed most deeply in exactly the traditions the industrial canon filed under "folklore." That is our thesis territory.
- Our audience (funders, systems-change practitioners) is actively looking for comprehensive thinking; range across traditions *is* the signal.

**Standards for doing this well (non-negotiable):**

- **Load-bearing, not decorative.** The tradition must do real argumentative work. If the sentence survives with the reference deleted, the reference is ornament — cut it or deepen it.
- **Name the tradition's own scholars and practitioners**, not only the Western academic who studied it. Cite Sophie Oluwole on Ifá, not just a comparative-philosophy survey; when citing anthropological work (Lutz on *fago*, Doi on *amae*), name the community and prefer sources from within the tradition where they exist.
- **Engage the actual content.** Summarize what the tradition holds and how it works, not just that it exists. "Indigenous knowledge systems understood X" without specifics is extraction wearing a compliment.
- **Verify everything.** Historical and cross-cultural claims get the same sourcing bar as studies — primary or well-attested secondary sources on the "sources to link" list. When possible, have someone from or close to the tradition read the passage before publication.
- **Two or three anchors per piece, maximum.** Each must be load-bearing. Abi's receipts work because every one carries weight; density without function reads as showing off.

## 4. Structural checklist (run before calling a draft done)

- [ ] Thesis stated as a contestable sentence within the first three paragraphs
- [ ] At least one transcript, scene, or worked example the reader can check themselves (marked as placeholder if pending — **never invented**)
- [ ] Stages/patterns/distinctions named (portable vocabulary present)
- [ ] Sections titled; each title could stand as a claim
- [ ] Strongest objection steelmanned and reframed
- [ ] 2–3 historical/cultural anchors, load-bearing, with at least one from a non-majority tradition where the material supports it
- [ ] Aphorisms surfaced — best lines end paragraphs or stand alone
- [ ] Every citation verified or converted/cut; "sources to link" list current
- [ ] Ending opens to a continuing practice; ring closed with the opening if one was planted
- [ ] Personal scenes fact-checked by the author (drafted-from-notes scenes are flagged until confirmed)
- [ ] Temperature consistent and intentional (default warm)

---

## 5. Anti-patterns

- Asserting significance ("this is profound," "hard to comprehend") instead of demonstrating it
- The five-jobs paragraph — multiple arguments compressed into one block, best lines buried
- Unverifiable citations kept because they sound authoritative
- Historical references as decoration (name-dropping a tradition without engaging its content)
- News-cycle reaction pieces; takes without frame replacement
- Hedged theses ("here's what I think this might mean...") arriving late
- Corporate-flat sentences at the moments the piece should peak
- Endings that merely summarize
